The project is done correctly on the numbers: an independent recompute from the raw run CSVs found **no numeric mismatch** anywhere in `writeup/writeup.md`, `README.md` or the 14 slides, and the one real traceability gap (about eight correct numbers that lived only in stdout or prose) has since been closed by `experiments/50_derived_checks.py`, which writes them to `results/derived_checks.csv` where all eight match. `make reproduce` now runs end to end from cache (exit 0, 22 s, 0 new model calls, tests pass), after a code review fixed the abbreviation-swap bug (abbrev now 2/77, verdict unchanged), cache atomicity, the model digest check, the figure title and Makefile dependencies. The harm citations in `reports/Failure B false premise design.md` are mostly right in substance but need **six concrete corrections**: one wrong patient count (8,358 should be 8,385), one imprecise effect size (aOR "1.5, P=.04" should be 1.51, 95% CI 1.02-2.22), two unsupported Kaiser figures (11% labelled, 63% higher cost), one unsourced "32 million", and one wrong-source delabeling citation (the Austin Health "194/200" link is a narrative review with no such number). ## Penicillin labels are common and usually wrong

The prevalence and "not truly allergic" claims hold, but the design doc cites them from the wrong layer of evidence. The Shenoy JAMA 2019 review says **"approximately 10% of the US population has reported allergies to... penicillin, with higher rates reported by older and hospitalized patients"**, and that clinically significant IgE- or T-cell-mediated hypersensitivity is **"uncommon (<5%)"** ([Shenoy et al., JAMA 2019](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2018.19283)). The design doc cites this through a CIDRAP news summary that adds "about 32 million Americans", a figure absent from the JAMA abstract with no source given ([CIDRAP](https://www.cidrap.umn.edu/antimicrobial-stewardship/allergic-penicillin-review-calls-deeper-probe)). The "5-16%, about 10%" line attributed to the BMJ paper is accurate. One note read the open-access full text and found "documented in 5-16% of patients" in the introduction and "reported by about 10% of patients" in the summary box ([Blumenthal et al., BMJ 2018](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.k2400)). The other note could not confirm it from the abstract alone, so the full-text reading wins. The 5-16% range is a background figure citing other studies, so Shenoy is the cleaner primary source. Setting matters: English general practice records show **5.9%** prevalence across 2.3 million adults ([West et al., J Antimicrob Chemother 2019](https://doi.org/10.1093/jac/dkz127)), and 11% of an MGH surgical cohort reported the allergy ([Blumenthal et al., CID 2018](https://doi.org/10.1093/cid/cix794)).

The design doc's guidance to say ">90-95% of labels do not represent true allergy" rather than "<10% true allergy" is the right call. The PALACE abstract states that **"fewer than 5% of patients labeled with a penicillin allergy are truly allergic"** ([Copaescu et al., JAMA Intern Med 2023](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2023.2986)), but in referred specialist cohorts positivity can run higher. In a Western Australian referral cohort, 12.3% (42/341) were skin-test positive ([Bourke et al., JACI Pract 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jaip.2014.11.002)), so the "<5%" belongs to the general labelled population, not to every clinic.

## Direct oral challenge is an RCT-validated delabeling route

The design doc's delabeling evidence is the weakest citation in the harm section. Its "97% (194/200) oral challenges were negative at Austin Health" links to a repository handle that resolves to a narrative review of direct oral challenge, with no 194/200 figure in its abstract ([Rose, Slavin, Trubiano, Expert Rev Anti Infect Ther 2020](https://doi.org/10.1080/14787210.2020.1792775)). The right replacement is primary trial evidence. PALACE randomised 382 PEN-FAST low-risk adults at six centres. Positive immune-mediated challenges were **1/187 (0.5%) with direct oral challenge vs 1/190 (0.5%) with skin testing first**, noninferior at a 5-point margin, with no serious adverse events ([Copaescu et al., JAMA Intern Med 2023](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2023.2986)). PEN-FAST, the rule that selects those patients, has an **NPV of 96.3% (94.1-97.8%)** at a score below 3 in external validation ([Trubiano et al., JAMA Intern Med 2020](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2020.0403)). Shenoy adds that "direct amoxicillin challenge is appropriate for patients with low-risk allergy histories" ([Shenoy et al., JAMA 2019](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2018.19283)). The 2022 AAAAI/ACAAI practice parameter is a real paper ([Khan et al., JACI 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jaci.2022.08.028)), but its recommendation text was not checked because no abstract was available. NICE CG183 returned HTTP 403 and is unverified.

This matters for the Failure B framing. The perturbation sentence used in `run_b_temporal_primary.csv` ("a negative challenge in 2019 showed the patient is no longer allergic to it") describes standard, evidence-based practice. A model that re-lists that drug is going against a settled clinical finding, not resolving a genuinely ambiguous chart.

## Label harms are real per patient but small in absolute terms

The design doc's BMJ numbers are exact. In 301,399 UK adults (64,141 labelled, 237,258 matched comparators, mean 6.0 years follow-up), the label was associated with **aHR 1.69 (1.51-1.90) for MRSA and 1.26 (1.12-1.40) for C. difficile**. Increased use of beta-lactam alternatives accounted for 55% and 35% of the excess risk ([Blumenthal et al., BMJ 2018](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.k2400)). The abstract's event counts (MRSA 442 vs 923; C. difficile 442 vs 1,246) put crude 6-year incidence **under 1% in both arms**, roughly 0.69% vs 0.39% for MRSA. That is an unadjusted figure derived from the published counts, so under CLAUDE.md rule 1 it should either be computed by repo code or explicitly labelled "derived from published counts". The surgical paper is the most useful per-patient sizing. Labelled patients received cefazolin **12% vs 92%** of the time and had **aOR 1.51 (1.02-2.22)** for SSI, "entirely mediated" by alternative prophylaxis, with **112-124 allergy evaluations needed to prevent one SSI** ([Blumenthal et al., CID 2018](https://doi.org/10.1093/cid/cix794)). The design doc's "8,358 patients" is a typo for 8,385. The decimals it gives (48.8/3.1, 34.7/3.3, 12.2/92.4) do not appear in the abstract, and its unconfirmed "51%" is simply the aOR of 1.51.

The Kaiser cohort is real but quoted beyond its abstract. Macy and Contreras matched 51,582 labelled inpatients 1:2 and found **+0.59 hospital days (9.9%; 95% CI 0.47-0.71)**, plus 23.4% more C. difficile, 14.1% more MRSA and 30.1% more VRE than expected ([Macy & Contreras, JACI 2014](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jaci.2013.09.021)). The "about 11% labelled" and "up to 63% higher antibiotic cost" figures, and the $60 million estimate seen in secondary press, are not in the primary abstract. A secondary source also reports that both authors had ties to ALK-Abelló, a skin-test reagent maker; this was not checked against the full text. The counterweight should stay: a systematic review of 8 studies concludes that "the impact of allergy labels on clinical outcomes was mixed" ([Wu et al., Can J Hosp Pharm 2018](https://doi.org/10.4212/cjhp.v71i1.1726)). The notes found no trial showing that delabeling itself lowers MRSA, C. difficile or SSI. All of the harm evidence is observational, and labelled patients are older and more comorbid.

## Relabeling after delabeling is measured at 7-36%

This is the evidence closest to Failure B, because it documents the exact error the model reproduces: a cleared allergy returning to the record. In Hong Kong's prospective HK-SPADE cohort, **15/88 (17%) delabeled patients were incorrectly relabeled within 12 months "despite a territory-wide unified electronic medical record system"** ([Shi et al., Ann Allergy Asthma Immunol 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.anai.2026.02.005)). At Mass General Brigham, **7% of allergy lists were not updated** after drug-challenge results in a 200-encounter sample drawn from 5,312 challenges (99% negative) ([Lo et al., Front Allergy 2022](https://doi.org/10.3389/falgy.2022.904923)). This is a cross-sectional persistence rate, not relabeling over follow-up. EHR allergy lists are noisy in general: one Chicago community hospital found 80.9% EHR-patient agreement, with 6.7% omissions and 12.4% incorrect entries among 270 patients ([Kabakov et al., J Pharm Technol 2019](https://doi.org/10.1177/8755122519840700)). The design doc's "460 reactions" for that study is implied by arithmetic (31/0.067 ≈ 463) and is not stated in the abstract.

The two notes disagree on Rimawi et al. 2013, and the disagreement comes from a DOI mix-up. One note looked up **doi 10.1002/jhm.2036** and found an abstract reporting only that 145/146 skin-test-negative patients tolerated beta-lactams, with no relabeling figure, so it marked "36% relabelled" as NOT FOUND. The other note looked up **doi 10.1002/jhm.2083**, Rimawi, Shah and Cook, J Hosp Med 2013;8:615-618. That abstract reports that **20/55 (36%)** skin-test-negative patients rehospitalised within a year had the allergy redocumented "without apparent reason", that the allergy was still listed in 21/21 long-term-care facility records, and that risk was higher with age over 65, LTCF residence and dementia ([Rimawi, Shah, Cook, J Hosp Med 2013](https://doi.org/10.1002/jhm.2083)). The two DOIs evidently point to two different 2013 Rimawi papers in the same journal, and the 36% claim lives in jhm.2083. The claim therefore holds under the correct DOI, **verified at abstract level only** (full text not read). One correction to the framing: the design doc itself does not cite Rimawi at all. The wrong DOI is in the research note `label_harms.md`, not in the design doc. The write-up should cite jhm.2083 if it uses the 36% figure, and should never cite jhm.2036 for it. Relabeling-specific letters (McDanel 2022, Norris & Bielory 2020 on merged EHRs, Olds & Chow 2024) were seen only as titles and should not be cited without reading them. No study measures relabeling caused by automated or AI tools.

## Conclusion

The audit turns the project's main risk upside down. The numbers, which looked most exposed under rule 1, turned out clean. The real exposure was in borrowed clinical citations, where secondary sources had brought in a typo, two phantom Kaiser figures and a wrong-source delabeling rate. The write-up has already moved onto primary-source references, so the design doc is now the only place those errors survive. It is an internal planning document, but it should be fixed so nobody copies from it later.

The relabeling literature also gives Failure B a stronger clinical anchor than the original MRSA framing. Human and EHR processes already re-add cleared penicillin labels in 7-36% of delabeled patients, so a model that re-lists 25% of explicitly cleared allergies in single-sentence perturbations is reproducing a known, measured clinical failure. That said, nobody has measured relabeling caused by AI, and the project measures per-chart re-listing, not patient outcomes. What remains before submission is mostly Madhavi's own work: filling `grader_right` in `handcheck_a.csv` (38 rows) and `handcheck_b.csv` (33 rows), cutting the write-up to four pages, and a final spot-check of references [1]-[6].
